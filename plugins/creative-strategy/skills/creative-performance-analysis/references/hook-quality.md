# Judging whether a hook is good

Use this when the question is whether an opening is a good hook: before launch, in an autopsy, or when choosing between candidate openings. It answers a different question from the [video section of inspecting ads](inspecting-ads.md#video), which checks that the body pays off the hook's promise and reads hook and hold rates. A hook can match its body and still stop nobody, and a rate shows that people stopped, not why.

Treat each check as an experienced-practitioner prior: a reason to look closer, not a measured result.

## 1. Stop the scroll, and stop the right person

An opening has to do two things at the same moment. It has to interrupt the scroll, and it has to interrupt someone who might buy. The second job is the one most often forgotten. An opening built only to be as strange as possible collects attention from people with no reason to buy, which is why a strong hook rate means little when the ad does not sell.

Picture a woman dragging a cheese grater across her phone screen while she talks to camera, and saying there won't be a scratch on it by the end. Anyone would stop, and for a moment nobody knows why she is doing it. But the strangeness is a screen protector doing its job, so the people with a reason to keep watching are the ones who worry about scratched screens. Grate an ordinary object for no reason and you still get the stop, without the selection.

The format notes ("Hook Rate ≠ performance") and the [qualified scroll-stoppers](#qualified-scroll-stoppers) below make the same point: a high Hook Rate can mean the ad stopped the wrong people, so judge an opening by the intent it pulls in. Hook Rate is defined in the [evidence rules](evidence-rules.md#hook-rate).

## 2. Why people stop: feeling, a gap and something at stake

Look for three ingredients. The opening makes the viewer feel something strongly. It leaves a gap between what they now know and what they want to know, big enough that they want the rest. And it puts something at risk, so they care how it ends. Great hooks can work without one of the three, but most carry all of them.

Each ingredient can sit in the words, the picture or the sound; it does not have to be written down. An opening with no text and an ambiguous image can work because of what the image tells the viewer.

| Opening | Feeling | Gap | At stake | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "A teenager at the petrol station looked at my tyres and asked if I had life insurance" (tyre check service, aimed at drivers not yet looking for one) | fear | what did a kid see in two seconds? | the narrator's own life | the line alone positions the product before it appears |
| "Knock on the door during my bath and you're out of the will" (bath soak, static) | shock at the threat | can a bath be that good? | a soak worth cutting your family off for | the copy: a static ad that runs on relatability and humour depends heavily on its words |
| "I've dyed my own hair for fifteen years and I'm embarrassed I only just learned this", over a clip of someone's hair being sucked into a vacuum cleaner | embarrassment at learning it so late | what did someone with that much experience just learn? | hair being ripped at | split: the words carry feeling and gap; the clip carries the stakes, though the ad is not about vacuums |

The third opening shows how much a picture can carry: the footage is there only to raise the stakes. It still shows hair, though, so the people it stops are the people the ad wants. A clip with no link to the buyer's world would stop everyone and select no one. That kind of opening can look like a cheap trick, and it is acceptable here as long as it still draws in the right person (section 1).

Several frameworks sharpen each ingredient.

- *Feeling.* [Mindstates](mindstates.md#the-nine-motivations) treats motivations as the emotional fuel behind a goal: nine feelings buyers pursue, such as Security (feeling safe and protected) and Competence (feeling capable and prepared). Name the one the hook's feeling serves. Fear about a crash serves Security; embarrassment at a gap in your own know-how touches Competence. A strong feeling that serves no reason this buyer has for the product is attention without selection (section 1). The label describes the ad's appeal, not the real buyer's mindstate, which needs customer evidence. To describe the feeling itself, the [CCS certainty/emotion map](ccs.md#emotional-entry-points-and-hook-structures) and the [Growthcraft valence/arousal map](growthcraft.md#211-emotional-quadrant-engineering-eqe) are both creative prompts with different axes; never translate one into the other.
- *Feeling and gap against the whole message.* Growthcraft's messaging ratio balance ([growthcraft.md](growthcraft.md), 2.1.4) counts emotion and intrigue as two components beside clarity, specificity and proof, and exists to catch "all sizzle, no steak". A hook can lead on feeling and gap; the ad as a whole still owes the viewer something specific and believable.
- *Stakes.* Under creative license, a hook may invent a scene or a person, like the teenager at the petrol station. An invented outside-world claim a viewer would take as real, such as a statistic or a named expert, needs a short end note in the delivered work. Whatever the stakes imply the product does (here, that a tyre check heads off danger) must be grounded in the product's records.
- *Channels.* The [qualified scroll-stoppers](#qualified-scroll-stoppers) below ask that the visual, spoken and on-screen hooks all point to the same buyer, topic and promise.

## 3. Why people stay: say it outright, or hold it back

After the stop, an opening keeps the viewer in one of two ways.

It can **promise**: put the substance up front and trust it to be enough. The viewer knows what they are getting and watches anyway because the thing is interesting in itself. If your glasses let colour-blind people see red for the first time, the hook should say exactly that, plainly, and the ad should show how the glasses do it. A title like "I Paid A Stranger $10,000 To Plan My Wedding" gives away the whole premise, and the premise is the reason to watch. When the content can carry it, say it plainly rather than cleverly.

Or it can **open a loop**: show the viewer that an answer exists and keep it back, so the question they now carry is what keeps them watching. A quick test for either move: after the opening, what does the viewer already know, and what are they staying to find out? If the honest answer is "nothing left", the promise has paid itself off.

Most loops contain a promise, but they are different moves, and a hook that cannot decide which one it is making loses its pull. Think of a line from pure promise to pure loop, with a few openings doing both. Two headlines about the same story show a closed promise and a hybrid:

- **"Oregon airport hires goats to keep grass short"** promises, then pays itself off. The outcome is already told, so there is nothing left to watch for, and naming one state shrinks who cares.
- **"Why Is This Airport Paying 3,000 Goats?"** promises an outlandish number and withholds the reason in a single question. The gap stays open.

The grater opening in section 1 promises as well: the claim that the screen will come through unscratched gives viewers a reason to stay and check, and the rest of the video is the proof.

Whether a plain promise will work depends on what the market has already heard. [Market sophistication](additional-lenses.md#5-levels-of-market-sophistication) says to state a claim nobody has made plainly and prove it, and to enlarge or sharpen a known claim that still sells; both suit a plain promise. Once buyers have heard every version ("they all say that"), a bigger promise stops lifting response, and the [mature-market response chooser](mature-market-response-chooser.md) rejects that bigger-claim reflex: the promise then needs a true mechanism, stronger proof, a sharper use case or an identity fit behind it, and if none of those is true, the fix is the product, offer, evidence or research, not the copy. Where buyers distrust the whole category, seeing themselves in the ad counts for more than any promise. The level is a working call from evidence, and one market can hold groups at different levels.

On the loop side, the [Curiosity Gap / Mystery Hook](#curiosity-gap--mystery-hook) note below requires the curiosity to resolve smoothly into the body. The CCS cognitive state check ([ccs.md](ccs.md), "Cognitive State Calibration") asks whether the hook joins a thought the viewer already has or demands a new one, and whether the body stays in the same register. Growthcraft's messaging ratio balance treats clarity and intrigue as separate components, the nearest account of this line in the frameworks. Whether the body pays off the promise stays with the [video section of inspecting ads](inspecting-ads.md#video).

## 4. What sinks an otherwise good hook

An opening with all of the above still fails if it does any of these:

1. **It leans on backstory.** A cold viewer was not planning to watch this video and may never have heard of the brand. For them the opening has to make sense with no prior context and must not leave them feeling they walked in halfway. The rule is about cold audiences, not people being retargeted.
2. **It warms up instead of starting.** Begin in the middle of the action and let curiosity build; don't explain what the viewer needs to know first. How much explanation is too much depends on what the audience already knows: people who lift weights already know what progressive overload means, so an opening that explains it wastes their attention.
3. **It is noisy.** Frantic editing (a zoom, a flashing caption and a sound effect all firing at once), too many things to look at, sensory overload: a confused viewer scrolls away. Aim for curiosity, never confusion, and when unsure, simplify.

The grater opening passes all three: it needs no context, it starts with the grater already on the glass, and it raises a question without muddling anyone.

Two separate lenses cover what the viewer brings. What a buyer knows about the problem, its fixes and the product is [awareness](additional-lenses.md#5-levels-of-consumer-awareness); how many competing claims they have heard is sophistication. The lifters example is about knowledge, so awareness is the lens that sets how much to explain, while sophistication sets how fresh the claim must be (section 3). A cold viewer can sit at any awareness level, including not knowing they have the problem. For that viewer the awareness guide's advice is to begin with a situation or mindset they recognise and then make the relevance clear, without assuming category knowledge: no backstory about the problem either, not just about the brand. The first frame can carry the action through the small real-motion cues listed as practitioner priors in the [qualified scroll-stoppers](#qualified-scroll-stoppers) below, as long as the device relates directly to the topic.

## 5. The duller the body, the more the hook must carry

Not every product comes with a dramatic demonstration. When the body of the ad is little more than a list, the opening has to work harder.

Take an ad whose body is a checklist of what to look for before booking a holiday rental. "Never book a holiday rental without checking these four things" works as a warning: it stirs fear, opens a loop (did I miss one last time?) and raises the stakes (a ruined trip). The ad leans on a loop because its body is not gripping on its own. Set it beside "Four things that make a holiday rental safe", which covers the same ground as good news: the warning holds people, because its feeling and stakes run higher.

The picture matters just as much. Plain screenshots of listings tell viewers a pitch is coming and give them no reason to stay. A father who walks into the rental and immediately pulls the sofa away from the wall makes them wonder what he is looking for. The words make sure the right viewers stay, and the picture can help too: a car boot full of children's bags may be aimed at families.

A warning is a cautious ("avoid more bad") frame. [Reading how an ad frames its benefit](gain-and-loss-framing.md) holds that neither approach is better and that fit with how the buyer already pursues the goal decides how well it lands; the cue in the ad does not reveal the buyer's approach, which that method finds through customer research. CCS (Phase 2, "Mass Desire Selection") lists conditions that may justify *testing* loss framing (high-regret moments, cautious buyers, tangible or financial losses, risk-averse audiences) and matching ones for gain framing (status or achievement products, optimistic buyers, aspirational categories, low-regret routine purchases). Neither is a default winner, so treat "the warning holds people" as the hypothesis the test settles.

## 6. The test under everything

Writing that informs or entertains is not the same as writing that sells. Selling copy reinforces a position in the buyer's mind and changes what they do. Hold every hook to that bar, including ones an AI drafted: anyone can now produce openings in seconds, so the useful skill is judging whether one will make someone buy, not whether it reads well. That judgment comes from writing to change behaviour yourself, and it pays off well beyond ads, from a pitch to a retailer to an email to a customer.

## Using it

- **Auditing an opening:**
  - Say who it stops and why that person might buy (1).
  - Note which of feeling, gap and stakes are present and where each lives; one can be missing (2). If it leans on a feeling, name the motivation that feeling serves (Mindstates). Check any invented claim and any product claim the stakes imply (creative license, product truth).
  - Place it on the promise–loop line and check it has not blurred the two (3). Before trusting a plain promise, check how worn the claim is (market sophistication).
  - Check for backstory with a cold audience, a slow start and noise (4). Judge how much to explain by what the viewer already knows (awareness).
  - Judge whether it carries enough for the body behind it (5). Check whether its gain or loss frame fits the buyer (gain and loss framing).
- **Choosing what a new opening must do:** CCS lists [hook structures](ccs.md#emotional-entry-points-and-hook-structures) and a register check for hook–body coherence ([ccs.md](ccs.md), "Cognitive State Calibration"). Use this page to judge them.
- **Diagnosing a struggling ad:** most of the time, a struggling ad's problem is the hook. Still test the competing explanations in [inspecting ads](inspecting-ads.md#explain-what-the-evidence-supports) (offer, audience, delivery, tracking) before blaming the opening.

## Notes carried from the video playbook and format guide

### Qualified scroll-stoppers

**Industry inference — experienced-practitioner prior:** useful first-frame patterns include justified provocation or anger, a close-up emotional face, topic-relevant gross-out imagery, an object flying into frame or rapid phone repositioning, and a familiar visual altered enough to cause a double take. These are credible starting points, not guarantees. The visual, spoken/audio and text-overlay hooks should all point to the intended buyer, topic and promise; otherwise a high Hook Rate can mean the ad stopped the wrong people. Start with the big idea, then choose the surface pattern.

### Hook Rate ≠ performance

**Hook Rate ≠ performance.** The widest-reach hook ("don't make the same mistake I made") can post the *highest* Hook Rate yet lose to a lower-Hook-Rate curiosity+benefit hook that pulls a higher-intent audience. Judge openings on downstream intent, not attention alone.

### Curiosity Gap / Mystery Hook

Visual signature: Opening creates information gap that viewers must watch to resolve. Incomplete revelation, surprising setup, unexpected juxtaposition. "Wait for it" structure. Payoff within first 15 seconds. Can be applied to many base formats — it's primarily an opening strategy rather than a complete format. Critical constraint: the curiosity must resolve into the body's register smoothly. A curiosity hook that resolves into a product pitch is the exact state-break the CCS warns about.
