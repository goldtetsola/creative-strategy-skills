# Reading how an ad frames its benefit

In [Mindstates](mindstates.md) the two approaches are Optimistic (promotion) and Cautious (prevention). These cues are built to match creative to the approach of a known audience, which is normally found through research, and they read picture and words together. There is no rule for an ad that shows none of these cues, or for weighing cues against each other.

## The idea

An approach is how a person goes after a goal. It is separate from the goal and from the motivation behind it. Take one goal, losing weight, and one motivation. An Optimistic person tries to make success as likely as possible, and might pursue it by eating more healthily. A Cautious person tries to make failure as unlikely as possible, and might pursue it by staying away from junk food. A health snack then has two honest descriptions. For the first person it carries extra vitamins and nutrients. For the second it has no trans fats and fewer calories. Same goal, same motivation, different frame.

The frame matters because of fit:

- A message that matches how the person already pursues the goal is more noticeable, flows easily and feels intuitive. The brand seems to understand them, and its claims are believable without extra proof.
- A message that does not match leaves people less engaged and the brand less trusted and less relatable. They seldom object. They delay the decision and do not come back.

Neither approach is better, and most people use both for different purposes. One example is a new mother who is gain-seeking about her career and cautious about decisions for her baby. Another is a young son who is gain-minded. The tactics that suit him are gain-framed: dessert for a clean plate, which got some vegetables eaten; being more like a superhero, as a second tactic for when that fails; and holding the ice cream he would earn, which makes success more likely. Warnings of illness or lost teeth did not work, because they were prevention-focused or handed him something he wanted anyway.

## Cues, grouped by what you look at

These lists are a few beginner strategies, and many combinations follow from them. They are not a complete set.

| Look at | Optimistic (promotion) | Cautious (prevention) |
|---|---|---|
| The benefit | pleasure; the benefits of success; the need for growth | pain; the cost of failure; the need for security or the possibility of rejection |
| The case for the product | explains why it makes sense and works, such as having the best scientists making it the best | stresses the scientific facts that prove it is the best |
| Feelings or facts | accentuates feelings | accentuates facts and reasons |
| Chances or stability | taking chances, making changes, seizing opportunities | stability |
| Speed or soundness | speed, progress, the whole product | accuracy, control, and the stability of each part of a system |
| Who is addressed | speaks to the individual as "you", which gives a sense of independence | refers to the group, "them", instead of "you"; people in this mode find comfort in the interdependence of people |
| Tone | cheerful | relaxing |
| Feelings to evoke | optimism, praise, nurturance, love, admiration, happiness, joy, excitement | security, conservatism, safety, calmness, familiarity, tradition, stability, pessimism, caution, relief |
| Delivery | animated gestures; faster speech, video and songs | reserved gestures; slower speech, video and songs |
| The picture | people being praised, working quickly and considering many alternatives; creativity, novelty, innovation; luxury, comfort, sophistication | people being criticised, or failure looming; or people making plans and being thorough, careful, thoughtful, accurate and harmonious |

The audience these cues serve differs too. Optimistic people value advancement, change and progress, expect things to go well, and want to feel happy. Cautious people work slowly and deliberately, want to feel prepared and less worried or stressed, stay with tried-and-true products that feel safe, and value conformity, tradition and security.

## How to read ads

Read an ad on the same cues, picture and words together. Four examples:

| Ad | Who is shown | Face, body, gaze | Words | Read as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| An image read as promotion | one person, which signals independence | happy and confident at a moment of achievement; active posture | "built", "efficiency", "raising your game": ego validation, pride, happiness | Optimistic |
| An image read as prevention | several people, which signals interdependence | the model faces the camera directly, arms crossed, looking worry-free; she seems secure and protected | "engineered", "security", "worry-free": risk avoidance; thousands of other customers use it: conformity | Cautious |
| Nike | a professional athlete | arms thrown wide, shouting, eyes above the camera; the wide arms and the rage prime promotion | "Just do it" | Optimistic |
| Under Armour | a professional athlete | arms at his sides, purposeful, focused, fully determined | the word "Armour" is itself a prevention cue | Cautious |

For Nike and Under Armour, assume buyers of both share the achievement motivation. Both brands promise to make you an achiever, so the approach can differ within one motivation. The two ads may look alike to a viewer and still feel different, and a viewer in a prevention mindstate is likely to lean toward Under Armour.

## Limits

**Choosing an approach**

- In most cases, choose one and build the creative from it. Others say a brand can speak to both. Changing is possible but hard, as with a pet food brand that had spoken to its buyers in a promotion way for thirty years. To marketers who say "we're a promotion brand": if the research shows their buyers want prevention, every promotion message works against their instincts.

**Finding out which one people use**

- Listen to how people explain their use of a brand or category to others, as gaining something or as avoiding something. Useful prompts are first-use stories, selling the idea to a best friend, collages and chosen images. In collages, "secure" is a cue word for prevention and "innovative" for promotion.
- Sort the responses into promotion and prevention piles, using a set of emotions, adverbs and adjectives that people naturally use in each state, and read the trend. Someone with fifteen promotion examples and four prevention examples generally approaches the category with promotion. People seldom fall wholly into one. This classifies consumers' responses, not ads.

**When the research does not settle it**

- With an even split, a new brand can pick whichever approach fits it best, since it has no existing messaging to clash with.
- A new brand with no research should use promotion, because an innovative new product benefits from people dwelling on its upsides.
- Large, established brands such as Coca-Cola and Kellogg's would most often benefit from keeping shoppers from trying other products, so lean them toward prevention.

**A default can be wrong**

- Prevention is the classic approach of almost all nonprofits, and can be dead wrong. A Greenpeace study found that older, higher-income donors responded better to how their support would improve the world, such as Germany's move from coal to solar creating thousands more jobs, and social media engagement rose sharply. That doesn't mean prevention never worked for Greenpeace. Promotion worked better for these donors, and both approaches can succeed.
